## Version A — Clinical motivation → reliability estimation → audit

Deep learning models for skin lesion classification are increasingly proposed as decision-support tools, but their deployment safety depends on more than accuracy on a fixed test set. A clinically usable model must also be able to signal when a given input falls outside the conditions it was trained to handle — a different acquisition device, clinical site, or patient population — so that a prediction can be trusted, deferred, or flagged for review rather than returned with unwarranted confidence.

The standard mechanism for producing this signal is distance-based reliability estimation: a test input is flagged as unreliable if it sits far, in representation space, from the class-conditional regions the model learned during training. Mahalanobis distance [Lee et al., 2018] is the most widely used instantiation of this idea in medical imaging, and cosine-similarity-to-centroid and non-parametric nearest-neighbor variants [Sun et al., 2022] are built on the same underlying premise: distance within a learned representation tracks how atypical, and therefore how trustworthy, a given input actually is.

This premise is a geometric assumption, not a guarantee, and it is rarely tested directly — least of all under training interventions that deliberately reshape a model's representation geometry. Shortcut-mitigation and domain-adversarial disentanglement training, increasingly recommended to stop clinical models from relying on spurious acquisition-site or demographic cues [Geirhos et al., 2020], are exactly such interventions: they exist specifically to change what a representation encodes and how it is organized. When distance-based reliability estimation and disentanglement training are deployed together — an appealing combination, since one promises to fix a shortcut and the other promises to flag what falls outside it — nothing in the existing literature establishes whether the second step still works once the first has changed the representation it operates on.

We audit this directly. Using a disentanglement dose-response ladder — three checkpoint families sharing one architecture and one representation, differing only in the strength of an orthogonality-based disentanglement penalty — we ask three questions in sequence: does the intervention change representation geometry; does that geometric change track distance-based reliability estimation quality; and, if it does not, is the information a reliability estimator would need simply absent from the representation, or merely inaccessible to distance-based geometry specifically. Our contribution is this audit and its result, not a new estimator: we show that a training intervention motivated by an unrelated goal can silently decouple representation geometry from the reliability signal built on top of it, while leaving the underlying information recoverable by a different kind of readout.

Across the ladder, condition number — a simple measure of representation geometry — shifts by two orders of magnitude with increasing disentanglement strength, while Mahalanobis-distance reliability estimation remains flat at approximately 0.40 AUROC, below chance, with no significant association to this or four other geometry metrics tested. The same result holds for cosine-similarity-based and k-nearest-neighbor scorers built on the identical embeddings. A supervised probe trained on those same embeddings, in contrast, recovers the relevant domain information at 0.73–0.81 AUROC at every disentanglement level tested. **Information can remain decodable while becoming inaccessible to distance-based reliability estimators.**

## Version B — Representation geometry → the distance assumption → audit

A learned representation is more than a set of features that support classification. Its geometry — how tightly points cluster by class, how well-conditioned the resulting covariance structure is, how close that structure sits to the Gaussian shape many downstream estimators assume — is itself something training shapes, and something training can change without moving classification accuracy at all.

Distance-based reliability and out-of-distribution (OOD) estimation methods are built directly on top of this geometry. Mahalanobis distance [Lee et al., 2018] scores a test point by its distance to the nearest class-conditional mean under an estimated covariance; cosine-similarity-to-centroid variants replace the covariance-weighted distance with a simpler angular one; non-parametric k-nearest-neighbor scorers [Sun et al., 2022] drop the notion of a class centroid altogether and use local density in the raw representation space instead. Despite spanning fully parametric to fully non-parametric, all three share the same premise: whatever geometric structure a representation has, a sufficiently faithful measure of distance within it will track how typical — and therefore how trustworthy — a given input is.

Because this premise ties reliability estimation to representation geometry by construction, any training procedure that changes representation geometry also changes, whether or not this is intended, the reliability estimator built on top of it. Domain-adversarial and orthogonality-based disentanglement training [Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015] are procedures designed explicitly to reorganize representation geometry — separating or suppressing structure associated with a nuisance variable such as acquisition site or imaging domain — and are increasingly used in clinical imaging pipelines for exactly that reason. Whether the geometric changes such training induces are the kind distance-based reliability estimation can still read, or the kind that severs the link between geometry and reliability without affecting classification accuracy, has not, to our knowledge, been tested directly.

We test it directly, using a disentanglement dose-response ladder that holds architecture and representation fixed and varies only the strength of an orthogonality penalty. We measure representation geometry directly (conditioning, class separation, multivariate normality); we measure distance-based reliability estimation quality using three structurally distinct scorers built on the identical embeddings; and, because a representation can fail a distance-based scorer for two very different reasons, we separately test whether the relevant information is present at all, using a supervised probe with no access to the training objective. This lets us distinguish "the representation lost the information" from "the representation still has the information, just not in a form distance-based geometry can use" — a distinction the existing OOD literature does not, to our knowledge, draw for a training-induced geometric shift.

We find that disentanglement strength changes representation geometry by two orders of magnitude on a standard conditioning measure, while three distance-based scorers spanning the parametric-to-non-parametric spectrum all report the same near- or sub-chance reliability estimate (~0.40 AUROC) regardless. A supervised probe on the same representations recovers the relevant signal at 0.73–0.81 AUROC throughout. **Information can remain decodable while becoming inaccessible to distance-based reliability estimators.**

## Version C — Shortcut mitigation → disentanglement → unexpected consequence → audit

Deep classifiers trained on medical images routinely learn shortcuts: correlations with acquisition device, hospital site, or other incidental image properties that predict training labels without reflecting anything clinically meaningful [Geirhos et al., 2020]. A shortcut that works on the training distribution can fail silently the moment acquisition conditions change — exactly the situation a deployed clinical model cannot avoid.

A standard response is disentanglement: train the representation, often with a domain-adversarial objective [Ganin & Lempitsky, 2015], to actively suppress the acquisition- or domain-specific structure a shortcut would exploit, while preserving the structure relevant to the clinical label itself. This is generally treated as an unambiguous improvement — the model becomes harder to fool with a shortcut, without, ideally, losing diagnostic accuracy.

But the same acquisition- or domain-specific structure that disentanglement is designed to suppress is also the structure a distance-based reliability or out-of-distribution (OOD) estimator — Mahalanobis distance and its relatives — needs in order to notice that a test input comes from an unfamiliar domain in the first place. A training objective built to make two domains geometrically harder to tell apart, deployed alongside a reliability estimator built on telling exactly that apart, is a combination with a plausible failure mode that, to our knowledge, has not actually been checked: fixing the shortcut may starve the reliability estimator of the very signal it depends on, silently, since classification accuracy on the training distribution is unaffected either way.

We audit this directly, using a disentanglement dose-response ladder in which the strength of an orthogonality-based disentanglement penalty is the only variable changing across three checkpoint families that share one architecture and one representation. We ask whether increasing disentanglement strength changes representation geometry (it does); whether that change tracks distance-based reliability estimation quality (it does not, for Mahalanobis distance or for two other structurally distinct distance-based scorers built on the same embeddings); and whether the domain information a reliability estimator would need is actually absent from the representation or merely inaccessible to distance-based geometry specifically (a supervised probe shows it is the latter). The contribution is identifying and characterizing this consequence directly, not proposing a fix or a new estimator.

Condition number — a simple measure of representation geometry — shifts by two orders of magnitude with increasing disentanglement strength, while Mahalanobis-distance reliability estimation remains flat at approximately 0.40 AUROC, below chance, with no significant association to this or four other geometry metrics tested; the same result holds for cosine-similarity-based and k-nearest-neighbor scorers built on the identical embeddings. A supervised probe trained on those same embeddings recovers the relevant domain information at 0.73–0.81 AUROC at every disentanglement level tested. **Information can remain decodable while becoming inaccessible to distance-based reliability estimators.**
